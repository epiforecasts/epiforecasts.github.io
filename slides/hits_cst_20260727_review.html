Review of the CST technical talk

How should we evaluate infectious disease forecasts?

Author

Review notes for Sebastian Funk

Published

July 25, 2026

Overall assessment

This is a strong choice for Tilmann Gneiting’s group. It is substantially more technical than the preceding HITS colloquium, with little redundant material. The central progression is compelling:

  1. Scores depend on the scale.
  2. Realised scores mix model capability with target difficulty.
  3. Apparent skill depends on the information set and reference baseline.
  4. Accuracy alone does not determine decision value.

The talk is best treated as approximately 30 minutes of prepared material within a one-hour slot, leaving substantial time for interruption and discussion.

Must fix

Do not confuse theoretical information bounds with fitted-model performance

The earlier version defined

\[ e(\mathcal I) = \inf_{F\ \mathcal I\text{-measurable}} \mathbb E[S(F,Y)] \]

and correctly stated that, for nested information sets,

\[ \mathcal I_1 \subseteq \mathcal I_2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e(\mathcal I_2)\leq e(\mathcal I_1). \]

It then showed a fitted seasonal model performing worse than the marginal null. Those claims cannot describe the same theoretical quantity. A forecast with calendar information can always ignore the calendar, so its optimal expected score cannot be worse than the optimum with no calendar information.

The empirical curves are performances of particular estimated model classes: AR(1), harmonic climatology, and a marginal model. They are model-dependent estimates or upper bounds, not the generalised entropies of the information sets.

Status: Fixed. The problematic COVID information-rungs slide has been removed. The talk now introduces the fitted models using French influenza, where autoregression performs best at short horizons and seasonality overtakes it at four weeks. The speaker notes explicitly distinguish fitted-model risks from theoretical information bounds.

Do not use “identifiable” to mean “no adjusted difference detected”

The title “Structure isn’t identifiable” was too strong and used identifiability in a technical sense that the result did not establish. Adjusted estimates close to one are not evidence of non-identifiability.

Prefer:

  • “Adjusted structural differences disappear”
  • “No detectable adjusted association with broad model class”
  • “The data do not support ranking broad model classes”

The analysis is observational and model-dependent. Adjustment may address measured differences in target difficulty, but it does not turn the result into a causal estimate of model structure.

The conclusion should therefore be:

We found no detectable adjusted association with broad model class; target difficulty and individual implementation mattered much more.

Avoid “adjusted models reveal zero difference”.

Status: Still requires checking in the visible slide title and takeaways.

Do not infer absence of information from failure to beat an AR model

The earlier claim was:

If an ensemble cannot beat simple past memory, no external signal is being extracted.

That inference is too strong. External information may be present but its gain may be offset by estimation error, model misspecification, combination inefficiency, or mismatch with the selected scoring rule.

Prefer:

We find no net predictive gain over the autoregressive reference under this evaluation.

Status: Fixed by removing “Do complex ensembles beat simple baselines?”.

Frame discrete-choice experiments as a research proposal, not a solution

A discrete-choice experiment could estimate trade-offs for one defined action, population, context, horizon, and presentation format. It would not recover a universal public-health loss function.

The elicited choices may depend on:

  • framing and probability presentation;
  • trust in the forecasting system;
  • organisational rules and resource constraints;
  • accountability for false alarms;
  • who bears costs and receives benefits;
  • whether the exercise is hypothetical;
  • whether the action can be revised later.

The slide should ask:

Could discrete-choice experiments identify enough of the relevant trade-offs to make decision-based evaluation operational?

Avoid saying that DCEs “complete the bridge”. Also avoid conflating decision loss, functional elicitability, and strictly proper scoring of a complete predictive distribution.

Status: Still requires softening in “But whose loss?”.

Remove the duplicated ending

The source previously rendered a section divider and a content slide both reading “That was hard”, followed by a generic thank-you slide.

Use one substantive closing statement instead:

There is no context-free answer to “which forecast is best”: the answer depends on the scale, task, information, baseline, and decision.

Then end on the productive unresolved question:

Can we elicit those choices well enough to evaluate forecasts for the decisions they are meant to support?

Status: Still requires fixing.

Important technical qualifications

Transformation and propriety

The statement that a transformed score remains proper “as long as the transform is invertible (strictly proper if strictly monotonic)” is muddled.

For a real-valued target, a measurable one-to-one transformation preserves enough information to transfer strict propriety. Strict monotonicity is a convenient sufficient condition for such a transformation, not a separate upgrade from propriety to strict propriety.

The slide should also make clear that evaluating \(\log(1+y)\) defines a different evaluation target and weighting of errors. Propriety does not select that scale; epidemiological relevance must justify it.

The second “Two problems” claim

The wording

If \(|r|\) is the prediction target, overprediction is penalised more than underprediction

is difficult to parse. If the intended claim is that equal positive and negative errors in growth rate produce asymmetric absolute errors on the natural incidence scale, say that directly.

Modelling scores as data

Be prepared to explain:

  • why the positive WIS outcome is modelled using a Gaussian family with a log link rather than Gamma or lognormal regression;
  • how heteroskedasticity and within-model dependence are handled;
  • the estimand under intermittent and selective model participation;
  • whether target difficulty variables could be consequences of participation or model-selection decisions;
  • whether the result is descriptive attribution or a causal claim.

The safe framing is an observational, descriptive decomposition of realised scores.

Pairwise comparison

The talk need not add a separate slide, but it should acknowledge the standard hub solution verbally:

Hubs address unequal coverage using pairwise comparisons on shared tasks. That produces a fairer ranking, but ranking is not attribution—and it still depends on the baseline used in the comparison network.

This also foreshadows the later baseline analysis.

Retrospective decision examples

Statements such as “all wrong” or “only model X was useful” require an event definition, decision threshold, lead time, and loss function. Otherwise they are retrospective visual judgements selected after observing the trajectory.

This is especially important because the section’s ultimate argument is that the decision and loss must be specified.

Baseline section

The original rank heatmaps did not communicate the practical consequence of baseline choice clearly. The revised figure is much better aligned with the claim:

  • baselines appear on the y-axis;
  • the x-axis is the proportion of hub models that beat each baseline;
  • coloured lines show horizons one to four;
  • COVID-19 and influenza are shown separately;
  • WIS is shown first and log-WIS is revealed as a fragment.

Proportions are preferable to counts because the eligible model total changes by horizon. For influenza there are 81 models at horizons one to three but 44 at horizon four.

The result uses the same pairwise scaled-relative-skill construction as the paper, restricting each comparison to shared forecast tasks.

Predictability section

The revised sequence is coherent:

  1. Show the French influenza observations alone.
  2. Reveal a prospective expanding-window climatology.
  3. Explain past memory versus calendar information.
  4. Compare marginal, seasonal, and autoregressive fitted models by horizon for French influenza.
  5. Show the full cross-setting handover figure.
  6. Ask which baseline should define success.

The prospective climatology uses only observations available before each prediction date. It therefore avoids the hindsight problem in the original week-of-year average.

The full handover figure should be described empirically:

  • the bubble marks where fitted autoregression is overtaken by the fitted seasonal reference, or fades towards the marginal null;
  • it is not an estimate of an intrinsic predictability ceiling;
  • “never crosses in the evaluated horizon” is safer than describing a pathogen as “pure momentum”.

Pacing

There are still many rendered slides for 30 minutes of prepared material. The talk can work if several slides are treated as optional and discussion is allowed to replace later detail.

A sensible default route is:

  1. Proper scores as the shared starting point.
  2. Scale choice and the ranking reversal.
  3. Forecast- versus target-generating processes.
  4. Scores as observational data.
  5. Adjusted structural result and target-difficulty drivers.
  6. French influenza: observations, prospective climatology, and crossover.
  7. Cross-setting handover.
  8. Baseline sensitivity.
  9. One MetroCast example and the threshold table.
  10. Formal decision value.
  11. Whose loss?
  12. One synthesis slide.

Slides that can be skipped when discussion runs long include:

  • the separate “Two problems” slide;
  • the untitled Ebola fan chart;
  • the hub screenshot;
  • one of the two MetroCast episodes;
  • section-divider slides.

Recommended opening

The existing opening questions are reasonable, but the technical tension can be stated more sharply:

Proper scoring rules tell us how to compare predictive distributions once the target and score are fixed. In infectious-disease forecasting, fixing those things is most of the problem.

The talk then examines the required choices: scale, comparison procedure, information and baseline, and decision loss.

Recommended closing

A concise synthesis is:

“Which forecast is best?” is incomplete without specifying the scale, the comparison task and baseline, the available information, and the decision and its losses.

The final question for this audience is:

We have mature tools once these choices are fixed. Can we fix them defensibly—and can we elicit the decision trade-offs well enough to evaluate forecasts for their intended use?
